# Behind the Print: How T-Shirt Printing Works

*The Craft of Turning Ink into Wearable Art*

Explore the craftsmanship behind printed T-shirts, discover the differences between screen printing, DTF and DTG, and learn what makes a print look and feel its best.

[IMAGE:J09-01]

Look closely at a printed T-shirt. In one design, the ink seems to follow the texture of the cotton. Another has a more distinct surface layer. A third is built from sharply defined areas of colour. These differences can reflect the method of printing, the materials and the artwork itself.

Three common approaches are screen printing, direct-to-garment (DTG) and direct-to-film (DTF). Screen printing uses a mesh stencil; DTG applies ink directly to a prepared garment; DTF transfers a design from a printed film. Each offers a different route from artwork to fabric.

## Screen printing: building an image in layers

A prepared stencil blocks selected areas of a mesh screen. A squeegee moves ink across the screen so that it passes through the open areas onto the fabric. Conventional multicolour work usually requires separate screens and careful alignment for each colour layer. The completed image must be cured according to the ink system.

Screen printing is particularly useful for repeated designs, bold shapes and carefully planned colour separations, though sophisticated detailed artwork is also possible. [FESPA's textile screen-printing guidance](https://www.fespa.com/en/news-media/quality-control-in-textile-screen-printing/) discusses the importance of stencil preparation, mesh selection and process control.

[IMAGE:J09-02]

## DTG: ink directly on the garment

A specialised inkjet printer applies a digital design directly onto a garment held on a printing platen. Pretreatment may be necessary depending on the equipment, fabric and garment colour. The print is then cured as required by the system.

This approach can reproduce gradients, photographs and detailed multicolour illustrations without a separate physical stencil for each colour. Many DTG systems are designed around cotton garments. The resulting texture depends on ink coverage, pretreatment and fabric, rather than the method name alone.

[IMAGE:J09-03]

## DTF: a transfer made on film

A DTF system prints the design onto a specialised film, applies adhesive and cures the transfer. Heat and pressure then bond it to the garment; the carrier is removed according to the transfer instructions. Because the artwork is transferred rather than printed directly onto the fabric, compatible materials can include a broader range than many cotton-focused DTG workflows. [Epson's garment printing guide](https://www.epson.co.uk/en_GB/buying-guides/guide-to-printing-on-garments) explains the differences and limitations.

## Comparing the methods

| Feature | Screen printing | DTG | DTF |
|---|---|---|---|
| Image transfer | Mesh stencil | Inkjet onto garment | Film transfer |
| Typical strengths | Repeated designs, defined colour layers | Gradients and detailed artwork | Detailed artwork on varied compatible fabrics |
| Material | Depends on ink system | Often cotton-focused | Depends on transfer system |
| Texture | Varies widely | Can be relatively soft | Can have a noticeable surface layer |
| Quantities | Often efficient for repeated designs | Can suit small orders | Can suit varied quantities |

These are general characteristics, not a ranking. The best option depends on the design, garment, desired finish and equipment. See the [custom T-shirt guide](/journal/custom-t-shirts-bangladesh-guide/) for the decisions a customer needs to make before production.

## What to inspect on a finished garment

Look at edge definition, fine detail, colour consistency and how the artwork sits against the fabric. Large uninterrupted graphics may feel different from designs that leave more fabric exposed. Correct preparation and curing influence durability; an attractive photograph cannot establish how well a print will withstand washing.

[IMAGE:J09-05]

The illustrated or conceptual comparison accompanying this article explains possible surface characteristics rather than reporting a controlled material test. For a real comparison, use printed samples made under known conditions.

Follow the care label. Inside-out washing, cooler water and gentle handling are common recommendations for many printed garments, but the correct treatment depends on the product. [Printful's care guide](https://help.printful.com/hc/en-us/articles/50263252811537-How-do-I-take-care-of-my-products) provides examples. Avoid ironing directly over artwork unless the manufacturer permits it.

Print quality is the outcome of many choices, not an automatic property of a technology. The artist's composition is one of them; [Beyond the Graphic](/journal/why-original-t-shirt-designs-matter/) explores that part of the process.
